\noindent The principal revisions are:

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Physics-model mismatch (new experiment).} The planner uses the nominal model while only the execution simulator is
perturbed, one parameter family at a time, by factors covering $\pm20\%$ and
$\pm40\%$. PushAround succeeds in \textbf{97.5\%} of the perturbed trials (\textbf{97.6\%}
including the nominal condition), whereas under the same $\pm40\%$ perturbations SL-Push (sim)
and DFS-WCCG reach \textbf{32.5\%} and \textbf{57.5\%} (Sec.~IV-A6, Table~III;
full grid in our reply to Reviewer~3). This study is new: the first submission validated
feasibility only under nominal physics.

\item \textbf{Density-scaling statistics (revised experiment).} The density sweep now uses \textbf{10 independent seed sequences} per
density instead of five. PushAround keeps \textbf{100\%} success across the tested
densities ($M{=}15$--$45$), with mean planning time rising from $2.5$ to $53.4$\,s, while the
comparison methods fall to $0/10$ at $M{=}45$ (Table~\ref{tab:dens-full}; the revised figure beside the original, with Wilson intervals, is in
our reply to Reviewer~3). The no-recursion ablation, which isolates recursive pushing, is reported alongside.

\item \textbf{Robot-team scaling (new experiment and mechanism).} Two mode-generation refinements were added to the shared generator, which
all policies use: bounded backtracking contact assignment and a temporary
per-task robot release. At $N{=}4$ the observed success rates change from $70\%$
to \textbf{100\%} for PushAround, from $0\%$ to $80\%$ for SL-Push (sim) and
from $80\%$ to $90\%$ for DFS-WCCG. (tables, figures and the pre-fix grid in our reply to Reviewer~3) PushAround
succeeds in $30/30$ team-size trials, with mean planning times of $23.9$--$30.0$\,s and mean execution times
of $97.3$--$110.1$\,s, versus $102$--$143$\,s and $176$--$178$\,s for DFS-WCCG (Sec.~III-C2, Sec.~IV-A6,
Table~III, Fig.~9, and Fig.~\ref{fig:resp-robot}).

\item \textbf{WCCG connectivity validation (new experiment).} Sec.~III-A2 derives the criterion from $W/2$ configuration-space inflation
with explicit non-convex decomposition. On 300 scenes WCCG agrees with the continuous
reference in all cases with \textbf{0 FP/FN}, whereas the two references agree in 296/300; a query
costs \textbf{1.07}\,ms against $1.36$\,ms (CC) and $243.1$\,ms (FR) (Table~I; details in our reply
to Reviewer~4).

\item \textbf{Formulation, guarantees and interfaces.} Eq.~(6) is now presented
as the realized clearance cost, accumulated in the node priority of Eq.~(14); the paper states explicitly that PIHS
is a feasibility-oriented heuristic search with finite sampling, bounded
lookahead, a non-admissible priority, and first-feasible termination, and makes
no global optimality or completeness claim. The optimized contact forces are
declared feasibility variables (not hardware setpoints), and coupled
object--object motion is explained as an outcome of the joint rigid-body
rollout in $\mathsf{EvalSim}$ (Sec.~II-C, II-D, III-C2, III-C3, III-D, III-E1).

\item \textbf{Comparison protocol and budgets.} Sec.~IV-A1 now states the
shared footprints, obstacle configurations, collision/reachability checks,
success criterion and simulator, and the shared per-trial budget
(\textbf{800\,s}, \textbf{20{,}000} control steps, \textbf{8} parallel workers).
Planning and execution times are averaged over successful trials, with failures
counted only in the success rate; the scalability curves rather than the main comparison assign the budget cap,
which corrects our earlier 400\,s figure for the same protocol.
\end{itemize}

All remarks have been carefully addressed; the main changes are listed above and
the detailed, point-by-point replies for every reviewer are attached in the
following pages. All materials, including the main paper with highlighted
changes, supplementary files and multi-media attachments, have been updated
accordingly. The per-trial records, resolved configurations and per-condition tables behind the new studies are included in the multimedia attachment
(\texttt{evaluation\_data/}).

\begin{shaded*}
\noindent \textbf{Q:}
{\itshape
This paper presents PushAround, a physics-informed hybrid search
framework for collaborative path clearing in environments containing
multiple movable obstacles. The problem is formulated within NAMO, but
specifically considers a team of mobile robots that actively rearrange
obstacles to create a corridor with sufficient clearance for a larger
external vehicle.

\textbf{Strengths:}
\begin{itemize}
\item Coherent end-to-end framework; the integration of WCCG, gap ranking,
pushing-mode generation, and physics validation is well designed. The method
jointly considers which obstacle to move and how to push it, while validating
candidate actions through simulation.
\item Recursive pushing; the planner can first clear movable blockers in the swept
region and then return to the original gap-opening task. Good feature!
\item Scalability: the method is evaluated with up to 45 movable obstacles, which
is a useful demonstration of scalability in dense clutter. The reported success
remains at 100\% at 45 obstacles, although the experiment uses five seeds per
density.
\item Strong simulation results and real-world validation: the proposed method
substantially outperforms the reported baselines in simulation and is also
demonstrated with two real robots.
\end{itemize}
}
\end{shaded*}

\noindent \textbf{A:}
We thank the reviewer for the positive assessment and for recognizing the
end-to-end integration, recursive pushing, dense-clutter scalability, and
hardware validation. The revision keeps the core hybrid-search structure unchanged and
strengthens the three aspects it touches: the recursive-pushing mechanism is
illustrated in its own figure (Fig.~4, Page~4), the dense-clutter evaluation now
uses ten independent seed sequences per density instead of five
(Sec.~IV-A6, Page~7), which addresses the caveat attached to the scalability
result, and the two-robot hardware demonstration is retained in Fig.~11
(Page~8) with the execution monitor specified explicitly. The responses below explain each point and quote the modified
manuscript text in blue.

The overlay composite of the two simulation scenarios (Fig.~7, Page~6) was also
regenerated, because one inset of the original panel did not match its snapshot;
Fig.~\ref{fig:resp-old-new-sim} shows the original and the revised versions side
by side.

\begin{shaded*}
\noindent \textbf{Q:}
{\itshape
Physics-model robustness is insufficiently characterized: The method
relies heavily on simulation-based feasibility validation, but
sensitivity to uncertainty in mass, friction, force limits, contact
dynamics etc. is not systematically evaluated.
}
\end{shaded*}

\noindent \textbf{A:}
We agree that the original evaluation did not isolate sensitivity to individual model parameters;
the hardware runs showed closed-loop recovery but did not separate them. The revision therefore
adds a planner--execution mismatch study separating model mismatch from re-identification (Fig.~\ref{fig:resp-mismatch} below):

\begin{itemize}
\item The planner always uses the nominal model and only the execution simulator is perturbed, so the
study measures the consequence of a model that is not re-identified rather than the benefit of
re-identification.
\item Obstacle mass, lateral friction, spin friction and the force limit are scaled separately by
$\{0.6,0.8,1.2,1.4\}$ ($\pm20\%$, $\pm40\%$) on the nominal seeds.
\end{itemize}

\noindent Across 17 conditions ($M{=}30$, $N{=}2$, 10 seeds each, 170 trials),
PushAround succeeds in \textbf{166/170 (97.6\%)}: the perturbed subset is \textbf{156/160 (97.5\%)},
i.e.\ 78/80 at $\pm20\%$ and 78/80 at $\pm40\%$, against 10/10 nominal. The four failures are one mid-execution planning failure (lateral friction $\times0.6$) and three
budget overruns (Table~R2). Mean replans rise from $2.3$ (nominal) to $3.9$ over the $\pm40\%$ conditions (up to
$8.1$ at force limit $\times0.6$), and mean execution time from $104.7$ to $159.6$\,s there.

\noindent For context, the two baselines were evaluated under the same protocol with five seeds per
condition. Under the eight $\pm40\%$ extremes, SL-Push (sim) succeeds in
\textbf{13/40 trials (32.5\%)} and DFS-WCCG in \textbf{23/40 trials (57.5\%)}.
Their mean planning times are $31.5$ and $229.1$\,s against $18.4$\,s for PushAround. Because the baselines use fewer seeds (5) and have lower nominal success ($3/5$), these results give
end-to-end context rather than a normalized degradation estimate. All 17 conditions and the baseline
supplements are listed per condition in Table~\ref{tab:mismatch-full} below.

\noindent \textbf{A:}
We agree, and we have both increased the number of independent runs and changed
how the results are reported.

\begin{itemize}
\item The density sweep now uses \textbf{10 independent seed
sequences} per density instead of five (7 densities $\times$ 10 seeds $=$ 70
PushAround trials). Denser instances are generated incrementally
within a seed while seeds are independent, so estimates at neighboring densities are paired but
seeds are not.

compares it with the original version, and Table~III lists $PT$/$ET$ as mean$\pm$s.d.; unsuccessful
trials are assigned the budget cap ($800$\,s planning, $500$\,s execution) before averaging, so the
panels show overruns rather than only successes. The complete grid is in
Table~\ref{tab:dens-full} and representative scenes in Fig.~\ref{fig:resp-density-scenes}.

95\% intervals in Fig.~\ref{fig:resp-reliability-r3}. At $M{=}45$ the PushAround interval is $[72.2,100]\%$ (10/10) and the baselines' $[0,27.8]\%$ from 0/10.

\begin{shaded*}
\noindent \textbf{Q:}
{\itshape
The experiments primarily use two robots. What happens when the
number of robots increases with the complexity of the task? How does
the search and the contact/force formulation scale to larger robot
teams?
}
\end{shaded*}



\noindent \textbf{A:}
The original mode-generation procedure became brittle as the team size increased: in preliminary
$N{=}4$ runs PushAround failed in 3 of 10 seeds (two planning failures, one budget timeout). The main
cause was that \emph{every} robot must receive a feasible contact in each pushing mode, which some
geometries cannot meet (a pushable face may be too small for all robots, or the pairwise
pusher-clearance constraint may eliminate one robot's remaining candidates), and which the
sequential greedy replacement may also miss even when a full-team assignment exists.

We therefore added two refinements inside the shared mode generator. First, if the greedy
replacement fails, a bounded backtracking search recovers full-team assignments it may have missed.
Second, if no feasible full-team assignment is found within the bounded search, one robot is
temporarily inactive for that push and holds its pose. Full-team modes stay preferred through a ranking penalty on reduced-team modes, and the
released robot is available again afterwards; the first refinement addresses search failure within the bounded search, the second the diagnosed cases
in which a full-team assignment is geometrically impossible.

Before the refinement PushAround solved 7/10 $N{=}4$ trials, afterwards 10/10 at $N{=}2,3,4$.
Both refinements are in the shared generator, so we re-ran both baselines on the same implementation;
their 40 trial logs record $20{,}890$ failed greedy replacements, $10{,}904$ backtracking assignments
(739 rescues) and $6{,}017$ robot releases, so the baselines exercise the added paths as well.

For scaling, an exhaustive joint assignment over $|\mathcal A|$ candidates per robot needs
$|\mathcal A|^{N}$ tuples, which the generator avoids: the greedy stage evaluates at most
$N|\mathcal A|$ substitutions, a bounded backtracking search of budget $B{=}2000$ follows if it
fails, and the reduced-team fallback evaluates the $N$ one-robot-excluded subsets, each with its own
bounded search. Explored assignments therefore stay polynomial, $\mathcal{O}(N^{2}|\mathcal A|+NB)$, and each
candidate is checked by a force LP of dimension at most $2N$ (Sec.~III-E2).

After the refinement all methods retain high success rates at $N{=}3,4$ with overlapping CIs, so we claim no monotonic superiority with team size. PushAround reaches $30/30$ successes at $29$--$30$\,s of planning and $97$--$110$\,s of execution; the full grid,
including the pre-fix rows, is in Table~\ref{tab:robot-full}.

What guarantees, if any, does the hybrid search provide regarding

\noindent \textbf{A:}
PIHS provides no global optimality or completeness guarantee: it samples a finite set of directions and contact modes, uses bounded lookahead, ranks nodes with a non-admissible heuristic and stops at the first physics-validated $W$-clear solution; a feasible scene may also need an unsampled direction or
deeper recursion, or exceed the computation budget. The returned schedule is therefore validated under the nominal planning model rather than
globally minimal: Eq.~(6) defines the realized clearance cost accumulated in the node priority of
Eq.~(14), and PIHS is not a solver of the underlying continuous hybrid problem. The reported node-expansion counts and
ablations characterize empirical search efficiency and solution-finding
reliability only.

We agree, and we provide both the requested comparison and an empirical

WCCG instead builds an embedded graph directly from the clearance-relevant geometry, whose
