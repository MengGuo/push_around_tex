\section*{To Reviewer 5}

\Needspace{8\baselineskip}
\begin{shaded*}
\noindent \textbf{Q:}
{\itshape
I thank the authors for their careful revision and detailed responses
to the reviewers' comments. This is a substantial revision, and I think
the paper has improved considerably. The additional experiments on
physics-model uncertainty, obstacle density, and larger robot teams
provide stronger support for the proposed method. The explanations of

Overall, I think the authors have addressed the main concerns raised in
the first round. The combination of geometric reasoning, collaborative

}
\end{shaded*}

\noindent \textbf{A:}
We thank the reviewer for the careful reading and positive assessment. The
five remaining points are addressed below with the corresponding final-manuscript
locations and excerpts.


\Needspace{8\baselineskip}
\begin{shaded*}
\noindent \textbf{Q:}
{\itshape
1. The response explains that the density experiments use a precomputed
mode table, while the mismatch and robot-team experiments use greedy
mode optimization. It also mentions a different scene realization for
the two-robot team-scaling experiment. I suggest including these
details in Sec. IV-A, since they help explain the different planning
times reported for M=30 and N=2. Please also clarify whether the time
needed to prepare the precomputed mode table is included in the
reported planning time.
}
\end{shaded*}

\noindent \textbf{A:}
Mode-table preparation is excluded from the reported planning time.
Sec.~IV-A6 now states that density experiments query the precomputed mode table,
whereas mismatch and team-size experiments optimize modes greedily. It also
reports the three nominal planning times at $M=30$, $N=2$ and identifies the
different scene realization used in the team-size study.
\par\smallskip\noindent\textbf{Final manuscript (p.~7, Sec.~IV-A6):}
\change{Density runs query a precomputed mode table (preparation excluded from $PT$);
mismatch and team-size runs optimize modes greedily. At $M{=}30$, $N{=}2$,
nominal $PT$ is $11.7$, $16.8$ and $23.9$\,s, respectively; the team-size
study uses a different scene realization of the same seeds.}


\Needspace{8\baselineskip}
\begin{shaded*}
\noindent \textbf{Q:}
{\itshape
2. Sec. III-C3 states that a physics rollout may be bypassed when a mode
has been validated before and the swept region is collision-free. A
brief explanation of the conditions for reusing this validation would
be helpful. In particular, please clarify which state and physical
parameters must remain unchanged, and how the successor state and costs
are obtained when the rollout is skipped.
}
\end{shaded*}

\noindent \textbf{A:}
Sec.~III-C3 now explains the reuse rule in the nominal model. A mode-table
entry can retain a validated state sequence in the object's body frame and its
cost increments. With matching relative states, contacts, twist, duration,
shape, mass, inertia, friction, and robot force limits, translation invariance
under uniform, isotropic nominal dynamics permits reuse after checking current
reachability and swept clearance. The transformed endpoint supplies the successor
state; the stored duration and effort increments supply $\delta_{\texttt{T}}$
and $\delta_{\texttt{J}}$. Uninvolved bodies remain unchanged. Small physical-parameter
differences make this reuse approximate. Sec.~III-D also refers to both
quick-pass reuse and fresh rollout when describing candidate evaluation.
\par\smallskip\noindent\textbf{Final manuscript (pp.~4--5, Sec.~III-C3):}
\change{For quick-pass, a mode-table entry can store a validated body-frame state
sequence and cost increments.}

\par\smallskip\noindent\textbf{Final manuscript (p.~5, Sec.~III-C3):}
\change{Transforming the endpoint gives $\mathbf{s}'$ and cached increments
give $\delta_{\texttt{T}}$, $\delta_{\texttt{J}}$; uninvolved bodies remain
unchanged. Small parameter differences make reuse approximate.}


\Needspace{8\baselineskip}
\begin{shaded*}
\noindent \textbf{Q:}
{\itshape
3. The new mismatch experiments are useful. However, the perturbed
trials reuse ten base seeds and change one parameter family at a time,
while the baselines use five seeds per condition. The response
acknowledges these limitations and the differences in nominal success
rates. I suggest adding a short qualification in the manuscript so that
readers can interpret the robustness comparisons appropriately.
}
\end{shaded*}

\noindent \textbf{A:}
Sec.~IV-A6 now states these qualifications explicitly. The perturbation
conditions are paired through ten reused base seeds, rather than representing
independent new scenes at each condition. The baselines use five seeds and
have nominal success $3/5$, versus $10/10$ for PushAround. The comparison
therefore describes end-to-end performance under the tested conditions,
rather than normalized degradation from equal nominal success. Joint
parameter errors were not tested.
\par\smallskip\noindent\textbf{Final manuscript (p.~7, Sec.~IV-A6):}
\change{These paired, single-parameter trials reuse ten base seeds (five for
baselines), whose nominal success is $3/5$ versus $10/10$ for PushAround.
Comparisons reflect end-to-end performance rather than normalized
degradation; joint parameter errors are untested.}


\Needspace{8\baselineskip}
\begin{shaded*}
\noindent \textbf{Q:}
{\itshape
4. In Sec. IV-B2, the text describes ``15.9 s of deviations'' due to force
limits, drifting, and slippage, whereas Fig. 11 reports execution time
as 77.1 $\pm$ 15.9 s. Please clarify whether 15.9 s is the standard
deviation or a separately measured recovery time. There is also a small
inconsistency in the response: the text on p. 4 gives 159.6 s as the
mean execution time under $\pm$40\% perturbations, while Table R2 gives
115.0 s for the aggregate and 159.6 s for the force-limit $\times$0.6
condition.
}
\end{shaded*}

\noindent \textbf{A:}
The $15.9$\,s in Fig.~11 is the standard deviation of execution time,
not a separately measured recovery time. Sec.~IV-B2 now reports
$77.1\pm15.9$\,s as mean $\pm$ standard deviation and describes replanning
separately.

We also correct the scope of $159.6$\,s in the previous response: it is the
mean for the force-limit $\times0.6$ condition, against $104.7$\,s nominal.
The $115.0$\,s aggregate in the previous Table~R2 is the equal-weight average
of the eight $\pm40\%$ condition means, each computed over successful trials.
The per-condition data are unchanged.
\par\smallskip\noindent\textbf{Final manuscript (p.~8, Sec.~IV-B2):}
\change{Execution takes $77.1\pm15.9$\,s (mean $\pm$ standard deviation);
online replanning compensates for deviations due to force limits,
drifting and wheel slippage.}

\section*{To Reviewer 6}

\Needspace{8\baselineskip}
\begin{shaded*}
\noindent \textbf{Q:}
{\itshape
The authors introduce PushAround, a hybrid search framework informed by
physics, aimed at cooperative path clearing among many movable
obstacles. Although the setting is rooted in Navigation Among Movable
Obstacles (NAMO), the focus here is distinctive: several mobile robots
work together to push obstacles aside and carve out a corridor wide
enough for a bigger external vehicle to pass. The pipeline brings
together WCCG-based geometric analysis, gap ranking, generation of
pushing modes, and physics-based validation in simulation. Experiments
span simulated scenes with as many as 45 movable obstacles, plus a
hardware demonstration using two robots.


\textbf{What I Liked}
\begin{itemize}
\item A well-integrated pipeline.
The pieces fit together convincingly: WCCG, gap ranking, pushing-mode
generation, and physics checking operate as one system. I particularly
appreciate that the planner decides both which obstacle to move and how
to push it, and that each candidate action is tested in simulation
before it is adopted.

\item Recursive pushing.
When a blocker sits in the swept region, the planner can move it first
and then resume the original gap-opening objective. This is a nice
design choice that makes the approach more flexible than single-step
pushing.

\item Evidence of scaling to clutter.
Handling up to 45 obstacles is a meaningful stress test. The method
reportedly achieves full success even at that density, though this
result rests on only five seeds per density level, which I address
below.

\item Convincing results in simulation and on hardware.
The approach clearly beats the baselines that are reported, and the
two-robot real-world experiment shows the idea can work outside
simulation.
\end{itemize}
}
\end{shaded*}

\noindent \textbf{A:}
We thank the reviewer for the assessment of the pipeline, recursive pushing,
and experimental validation. The two concerns are addressed by the
execution-side mismatch study and the ten-seed density evaluation included in
version~2. We identify their settings, results, and limitations below; this
final revision adds protocol qualifications without introducing new experiments.


\Needspace{8\baselineskip}
\begin{shaded*}
\noindent \textbf{Q:}
{\itshape
\textbf{Concerns}

Unclear robustness to model error.
Because the whole approach leans on simulation to judge whether a push
is feasible, its reliability depends on how accurate the physical model
is. Yet there is no systematic study of how errors or uncertainty in
mass, friction, force limits, or contact behavior affect performance.
For a method whose central claim is physics-informed planning, this is
an important omission.
}
\end{shaded*}

\noindent \textbf{A:}
The manuscript includes a systematic planner--execution mismatch study in
Sec.~IV-A6 and Table~III (p.~7). The planner retains nominal physics while
only the execution simulator is perturbed. Mass, lateral friction, spinning
friction, and robot force limits are varied one family at a time by factors
$0.6$, $0.8$, $1.2$, and $1.4$. With ten base seeds per condition, PushAround
succeeds in $156/160$ perturbed trials ($97.5\%$).

The final manuscript now states the paired-seed design, unequal sample counts
and nominal success rates, and the absence of joint-parameter tests. The two
friction parameters probe contact behavior within the tested model.
\par\smallskip\noindent\textbf{Existing manuscript result (p.~7, Sec.~IV-A6):}
\textbf{(III) Physics mismatch:} the planner keeps the nominal model and only
the execution simulator is perturbed, one parameter at a time.


\Needspace{8\baselineskip}
\begin{shaded*}
\noindent \textbf{Q:}
{\itshape
Thin statistical evidence at high complexity.
The densest scenarios are tested with relatively few independent runs.
A perfect success rate over five seeds is encouraging but offers
limited confidence, and it undercuts the scalability argument somewhat.
}
\end{shaded*}

\noindent \textbf{A:}
The density evaluation was expanded from five to ten independent base seeds
per density in version~2. Sec.~IV-A6, Table~III, and Fig.~10 (p.~7) report this
ten-seed evaluation for $M=15$--$45$. At the highest tested density,
$M=45$, PushAround succeeds in $10/10$ trials. Table~III reports timing
means and standard deviations; the timing panels of Fig.~10 include error
bars. The observed $10/10$ is a finite-sample result, not a guarantee of
universal success in dense scenes.
\par\smallskip\noindent\textbf{Existing manuscript result (p.~7, Sec.~IV-A6):}
\textbf{(I) Obstacle density:} with two robots, $10$ trials per density,
PushAround keeps $100\%$ success at all tested densities ($M{=}15$ to $45$), planning in $2.5$--$53.4$\,s; at $M{=}45$ none of the evaluated baselines solves any trial
and the no-recursion ablation solves $7/10$.


\Needspace{8\baselineskip}
\begin{shaded*}
\noindent \textbf{Q:}
{\itshape
This is a solid and well-presented paper. Combining geometric
reasoning, recursive pushing, and physics-informed search is an
interesting direction, and the hardware validation adds credibility. My
main reservations are the missing sensitivity analysis for physical
parameters and the small number of trials in the densest settings.
Resolving these points, and answering the questions above, would
considerably strengthen the submission.
}
\end{shaded*}

\noindent \textbf{A:}
We thank the reviewer for the constructive comments. The locations above
identify the existing sensitivity and expanded density studies; the added
protocol qualifications make their scope and statistical limitations explicit.
