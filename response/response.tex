\noindent The principal revisions are:

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{(Pages 2 and 7, Secs.~I-B and IV-A6)} \textbf{Physics-model robustness (new experiment).} Planning uses the
nominal model; only the execution simulator is perturbed, one parameter family

\item \textbf{(Page 7, Sec.~IV-A6 and Fig.~9)} \textbf{Obstacle-density statistics (revised experiment).} The density

\subsection*{R3.1 Physics-model robustness is insufficiently characterized}
%==============================
\begin{shaded*}
\noindent \textbf{Q:}
{\itshape
The method relies heavily on simulation-based feasibility validation, but
sensitivity to uncertainty in mass, friction, force limits, contact
dynamics etc.\ is not systematically evaluated.
}
\end{shaded*}

\noindent \textbf{A:}
We agree that this was the most substantive gap, because physics validation is
the distinguishing element of the method. The original hardware experiments
demonstrated closed-loop recovery but did not \emph{isolate} sensitivity to
individual model parameters. The revision therefore adds a dedicated
planner--execution mismatch study with two properties that make the claim
falsifiable:

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Mismatch, not retuning.} The planner always uses the nominal
model; \emph{only} the execution simulator is perturbed. The experiment
therefore measures the consequence of an incorrect planning model rather than
the benefit of re-identifying it.
\item \textbf{One family at a time, paired seeds.} Obstacle mass, lateral
friction, spinning friction and the robot force limit are scaled separately by
$\{0.6,0.8,1.2,1.4\}$, i.e.\ $\pm20\%$ and $\pm40\%$, using the same scene
seeds as the nominal condition.
\end{itemize}

\noindent\textbf{Result.} Over 17 conditions ($M{=}30$, $N{=}2$, 10 seeds
each, 170 trials) PushAround succeeds in \textbf{166/170 (97.6\%)}, and
importantly the success rate does \emph{not} degrade with the perturbation
magnitude: \textbf{78/80 (97.5\%)} at $\pm20\%$ and \textbf{78/80 (97.5\%)} at
$\pm40\%$, versus 10/10 nominal. The four failures are one mid-execution
planning failure (lateral friction $\times0.6$) and three trials that exceeded
the per-trial budget (spin friction $\times1.2$, force limit $\times0.6$ and
$\times1.2$). Replanning absorbs the remainder: mean replans rise from $2.3$
(nominal) to $3.9$ ($\pm40\%$ average; up to $8.1$ under force limit
$\times0.6$), where the weaker pushing force shortens the reachable force range
and lengthens execution from $104.7$\,s to $159.6$\,s.

\noindent\textbf{Baseline comparison (added in this revision).} To show that
this robustness is a property of the method rather than of the scene, we ran
SL-Push (sim) and DFS-WCCG under the same protocol (nominal planning,
execution-only perturbation, 5 seeds per condition). Under the identical
$\pm40\%$ perturbations, SL-Push (sim) attains \textbf{13/40 (32.5\%)} --- with
$0\%$ under lateral friction $\times1.4$ and force limit $\times0.6$ --- and
DFS-WCCG \textbf{23/40 (57.5\%)}, while requiring ${\sim}12\times$ more
planning time (mean $229$\,s versus $18$\,s). Both baselines are already weaker
at nominal physics (3/5 each versus 10/10), which is consistent with the main
comparison in Table~II.

\subsection*{R3.3 Scaling of the search and of the contact/force formulation with team size}
%==============================
\begin{shaded*}
\noindent \textbf{Q:}
{\itshape
The experiments primarily use two robots. What happens when the number of
robots increases with the complexity of the task? How does the search and the
contact/force formulation scale to larger robot teams?
}
\end{shaded*}

\noindent \textbf{A:}
This question turned out to be more productive than expected. It exposed a
mode-generation bottleneck, we fixed it, and the re-run --- performed on
\emph{both} PushAround and the baselines with identical code --- shows that the
fix lives in the shared mode generator rather than being a PushAround-only
behaviour. The corrected comparison is reported below.

\textbf{(a) Complexity.} The implementation never enumerates joint contact
tuples. With $|\mathcal A|$ reachable contact candidates per robot, exhaustive
assignment would require $|\mathcal A|^N$ tuples; instead one greedy replacement
sweep tests at most $N|\mathcal A|$ single-contact substitutions, each solving
the force LP whose decision vector has dimension $2N$. Larger teams therefore
enlarge the feasible wrench set but also increase contact-assignment and LP
cost. Sec.~III-E2 states this explicitly.

\textbf{(b) Bottleneck, fix, and who benefits.} The preliminary $N{=}4$ runs
failed in 3 of 10 seeds (2 planning failures, 1 budget timeout). Diagnosis: for
some push tasks \emph{no} full-team contact assignment exists --- small
pushable faces, or the pairwise pusher-clearance constraint rejecting every
remaining candidate of one robot --- and the legacy greedy replacement was
additionally sensitive to robot ordering. We added two mode-generation-local
refinements that leave global planning, task sampling and the search tree
unchanged: (i) \emph{bounded backtracking} contact assignment and (ii) a
\emph{temporary per-task robot release} (up to one robot is excluded for that
single push and holds its pose, a penalty is charged during mode ranking so
full-team modes are preferred, and the full team is re-available for every
later task).

Both refinements sit in the shared mode generator, so the baselines inherit
them too --- which is why the baseline cells were re-run on the same code.
Instrumenting the 40 baseline trial logs (4 cells $\times$ 10 seeds) counts
$20{,}890$ failed greedy replacements, $10{,}904$ backtracking assignments
(rescuing $739$ of them) and $6{,}017$ per-task robot releases. The effect is
therefore not PushAround-specific:

\subsection*{R3.4 Solution-quality guarantees of the hybrid search}
%==============================
\begin{shaded*}
\noindent \textbf{Q:}
{\itshape
What guarantees, if any, does the hybrid search provide regarding solution
quality?
}
\end{shaded*}

\noindent \textbf{A:}
Thank you for insisting on this. No global optimality or completeness guarantee
is claimed, and the revision now says so explicitly in three places rather than
leaving it implicit.

PIHS samples a finite set of pushing directions and contact modes, uses bounded
gap lookahead, ranks nodes with a heuristic best-first priority whose
remaining-cost term is \emph{not} required to be admissible, and terminates at
the first physics-validated $W$-clear solution. The returned schedule is
therefore a feasible schedule, not a globally minimal one. Consistently,
Eq.~(5) is presented as the clearance cost that ranks candidate schedules;
it is also stated that the objective couples obstacle selection, mode
generation, and switching into a hybrid combinatorial--continuous problem that
the implemented search does not solve to global optimality.

\noindent\textbf{Changes in the revised manuscript:}

\subsection*{R3.6 Detection of simulation--execution discrepancies}

\noindent \textbf{Q:}
{\itshape

In the hardware experiments, motion capture provides robot and object poses. A
push is declared to deviate --- and replanning is triggered from the observed

routinely recoverable by the subsequent tasks. No onboard force sensing or
mass/friction estimation is required.

\subsection*{R4.1 WCCG connectivity: justification and C-space comparison}
%==============================
\begin{shaded*}
\noindent \textbf{Q:}
{\itshape
The WCCG connectivity condition is important since it is used throughout the
search and also determines termination \dots{} the paper would benefit from a
little more justification here, or perhaps a comparison with a standard
configuration-space connectivity check. This seems especially relevant when the
obstacles are non-convex.
}
\end{shaded*}

\noindent \textbf{A:}
We agree, and we provide both a derivation and the suggested empirical check.

\textbf{(a) Why the same-face test is the right query.} For a vehicle whose
footprint is enclosed by a disk of radius $W/2$, inflating every obstacle
boundary by $W/2$ turns any pair of boundary components whose closest distance
is below $W$ into \emph{overlapping} barriers in the configuration space of the
vehicle centre. A WCCG bridge--bridge edge records exactly such a
non-traversable bottleneck, and centroid--bridge edges attach the bottleneck to
the adjacent free regions. Consequently, ``start and goal lie in the same
planar face'' is equivalent to connectivity in the complement of the inflated
geometry, i.e.\ to the $W$-clearance condition. Non-convex obstacles require care, which
is why the construction first performs a clearance-relevant convex
decomposition and then inserts bridges between the resulting
components/segments.

\textbf{(b) Empirical validation on 300 scenes.} Because degenerate geometry
and decomposition choices still matter in practice, we also compare WCCG
against two independent references on 300 random scenes stratified into convex,
mixed, and non-convex obstacles: a continuous configuration-space (CC)
reference and a rasterized reference (FR) at $0.02$\,m, verified at $0.01$\,m.
CC and FR disagree in \textbf{4/300} cases, all of which are raster-unstable
near narrow or tangential contacts. On all 300 scenes, WCCG agrees with the
continuous reference and produces \textbf{zero false positives and zero false
negatives} --- a false positive would be the dangerous case, as it could
terminate PIHS prematurely. WCCG additionally answers a query in
\textbf{1.07}\,ms on average, comparable to CC ($1.36$\,ms) and
$\mathbf{227\times}$ faster than FR ($243.1$\,ms), while supplying the gap
graph that PIHS consumes.

\subsection*{R4.3 Hardware force interface and coupled object motion}
%==============================
\begin{shaded*}
\noindent \textbf{Q:}
{\itshape
It is not completely clear how the optimized contact forces are used on the
hardware \dots{} whether these forces are actually commanded or are mainly used
to check feasibility. Related to this, I am also not sure whether coupled motion
between multiple objects \dots{} is explicitly considered during planning or is
mostly handled by the physics simulation.
}
\end{shaded*}

\noindent \textbf{A:}
Both ambiguities are now removed explicitly.

\textbf{Forces are feasibility variables.} For a candidate contact tuple, the
LP decides whether the robots can generate a wrench compatible with the desired
object motion within the modelled force limits, friction cone, and unilateral
contact constraints. The optimized vector is therefore used to \emph{certify
and rank} wrench feasibility; it is not sent to the hardware. The Mecanum UGVs
receive planar velocity commands (\texttt{geometry\_msgs/Twist}) and track the
mode-defined contact poses and a short forward object-motion reference using
motion-capture feedback.

\textbf{Coupled motion is an outcome of the rollout.} A high-level strategy
names one directly targeted obstacle, but $\mathsf{EvalSim}$ advances all
robots and all movable bodies jointly; object--object contacts are resolved by
the physics engine and the complete successor scene is returned to the search.
The adjacent T-shaped block moved in hardware Scenario~2 is exactly this
contact-induced motion, not a separately scripted task --- which is also why
the revision keeps the coupled rollout as the source of physical validation.

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{(Page 2, Sec.~II-C)} \change{The force vector $\mathbf{u}_m$ serves as a
feasibility variable to certify wrench generation under the modeled contact and
actuation constraints rather than a directly commanded control input.}
\item \textbf{(Page 4, Sec.~III-C3)} \change{The rollout jointly simulates all robots and movable
bodies, thereby capturing contact-induced object interactions.} and
\change{$\mathbf{u}_m^k$ certifies wrench feasibility while hardware tracks the
contact geometry from pose/velocity commands.}
\item \textbf{(Pages 5 and 7, Secs.~III-E1 and IV-B1)} Sec.~III-E1 and Sec.~IV-B1 describe the velocity-level controller and the
motion-capture feedback loop.
\item \textbf{(Page 8, Sec.~IV-B2)} \change{This neighboring-object motion is not a separately

\section*{Statistical reliability, provenance and limitations}
\label{sec:reliability}
%========================================

\noindent\textbf{Independence and pairing.} Density scenes are nested within a
seed (denser instances extend the same base layout) but independent across
seeds; the mismatch study reuses the same scene seeds across conditions, so
perturbed cells are paired with their nominal counterpart and the comparison is
not confounded by scene difficulty.

\noindent\textbf{Failure accounting.} Every suite stores, per trial, the status
(\texttt{task\_done}, \texttt{plan\_fail}, \texttt{timeout},\allowbreak
\texttt{timeout\_pruned}, \texttt{error}), the planning and execution time,
the number of replans and push tasks, and the simulation count. Planning-time
statistics cap unsuccessful trials at the timeout, which prevents plan failures
from appearing artificially fast --- a bias that would otherwise favour the
baselines in dense clutter. The complete compositions are listed in
the appendix tables.

\noindent\textbf{Code versions (disclosed for completeness).} The main
comparison, the density sweep and the mismatch study were produced with the code
revision that predates the mode-generation refinements described in Sec.~R3.3.
Both sides of each of those comparisons use that same revision, and the
refinements can only help PushAround, so those numbers are conservative. The
whole robot-team study --- PushAround \emph{and} both baselines --- was re-run
on the current revision (experiment-repo commit \texttt{43c8d62}, descending
from the shared fix \texttt{fc857a8}, with identical scenes, budgets and seeds),
so the team-size comparison uses a single code version throughout.

\noindent\textbf{Data availability.} Per-trial records (run status, planning and
execution time, replan count, push count, simulation count and failure reason)
exist for every experiment reported above, and all tables and figures in this
letter are computed directly from them. These records, the configuration files
of every study and a script that regenerates all figures of this letter from
them accompany this submission as part of the multimedia material; the complete
per-condition results are also reproduced in the appendix tables below.

\noindent\textbf{Known limitations.} (i) The mismatch baseline supplements use 5
seeds per cell (the robot-team cells use 10 for every policy), so their
intervals are wide; the qualitative separation from
PushAround is nevertheless far larger than the interval widths
(Fig.~\ref{fig:resp-reliability}). (ii) The WCCG validation uses single-obstacle-type
scenes with 10 movable obstacles; scaling this check to denser scenes is left
to future work. (iii) Hardware results remain a demonstration over 20 runs and
two scenarios, without a hardware baseline, as no comparable multi-robot
clearing system was available to us.
