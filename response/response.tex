\noindent The principal revisions are:

\begin{itemize}
\item The planner uses the nominal model while the execution simulator perturbs
one parameter family at a time (obstacle mass, lateral friction, spinning
friction, or robot force limit) by factors $\{0.6,0.8,1.2,1.4\}$, i.e.,
$\pm20\%$ and $\pm40\%$. PushAround succeeds in \textbf{156/160} perturbed
trials (\textbf{97.5\%}); including the nominal condition, it succeeds in
\textbf{166/170} trials (\textbf{97.6\%}). Under the same $\pm40\%$
perturbations, SL-Push (sim) succeeds in \textbf{13/40} trials (32.5\%) and
DFS-WCCG in \textbf{23/40} trials (57.5\%). DFS-WCCG also requires about
$12\times$ more planning time than PushAround (Sec.~IV-A6, Table~III,
Fig.~\ref{fig:resp-mismatch}).

\item The density sweep now uses \textbf{10 independent seed sequences} per
density instead of five. PushAround maintains \textbf{100\%} success for every
$M\in[15,45]$, with mean planning time increasing from $2.5$ to $53.4$\,s;
the comparison methods fall to $0/10$ at $M{=}45$ (the no-recursion ablation
achieves $7/10$). Dispersion and Wilson confidence intervals are now reported
(Sec.~IV-A6, Table~III, Fig.~\ref{fig:resp-reliability}).

\item Two mode-generation refinements were added: bounded backtracking contact
assignment and a temporary per-task robot release when no full-team assignment
exists. Both are in the shared mode generator and were used for all policies.
At $N{=}4$, the observed success rates change from $70\%$ to \textbf{100\%}
for PushAround, from $0\%$ to $80\%$ for SL-Push (sim), and from $80\%$ to
$90\%$ for DFS-WCCG. PushAround succeeds in all $30$ team-size trials; at
$N{=}3,4$ it uses $29$--$30$\,s of planning and $97$--$110$\,s of execution,
whereas DFS-WCCG uses $102$--$143$\,s of planning and $176$--$178$\,s of
execution, and SL-Push (sim) uses $172$--$193$\,s of execution and $20$--$31$
pushes (Sec.~III-C2, Sec.~IV-A6, Table~III, Fig.~9, and
Fig.~\ref{fig:resp-robot}).

\item Sec.~III-A2 now derives the criterion from $W/2$ configuration-space
inflation with explicit non-convex decomposition. On 300 scenes, WCCG agrees
with the continuous reference in \textbf{300/300} cases with \textbf{0 FP/FN};
the continuous and rasterized references agree in 296/300 cases. In the timing
harness, WCCG averages \textbf{1.07}\,ms per query versus $1.36$\,ms for CC and
$243.1$\,ms for FR (an implementation-level ratio of $227\times$ versus FR)
(Table~I and Fig.~\ref{fig:resp-wccg}).

\item Eq.~(5) is now presented
as the schedule cost guiding the search; the paper states explicitly that PIHS
is a feasibility-oriented heuristic search with finite sampling, bounded
lookahead, a non-admissible priority, and first-feasible termination, and makes
no global optimality or completeness claim. The optimized contact forces are
declared feasibility variables (not hardware setpoints), and coupled
object--object motion is explained as an outcome of the joint rigid-body
rollout in $\mathsf{EvalSim}$ (Sec.~II-C, II-D, III-C2, III-C3, III-D, III-E1).

\item Sec.~IV-A1 now states the
shared footprints, obstacle configurations, collision/reachability checks,
success criterion and simulator, and the shared per-trial budget
(\textbf{800\,s} timeout, \textbf{20{,}000} control steps, \textbf{8} parallel
workers), with unsuccessful trials assigned the 800\,s cap in planning-time
statistics. The previous 400\,s statement is corrected.
\end{itemize}

The detailed responses below provide the manuscript locations and revised text
for each reviewer comment. The supplementary evaluation package has also been
updated with the per-trial records and configuration files backing the reported
statistics.

\noindent \textbf{Q:}
{\itshape
This paper presents PushAround, a physics-informed hybrid search
framework for collaborative path clearing in environments containing
multiple movable obstacles. The problem is formulated within NAMO, but
specifically considers a team of mobile robots that actively rearrange
obstacles to create a corridor with sufficient clearance for a larger
external vehicle.
}
\end{shaded*}

\noindent \textbf{A:}
We sincerely thank the reviewer for the positive assessment and, in particular,
for recognizing the end-to-end integration, recursive pushing, dense-clutter
scalability, and hardware validation. The revision addresses the two stated
weaknesses and the four follow-up questions without changing the central
planning framework. The detailed responses below explain these revisions
point by point and provide the exact modified text highlighted in blue. The
supplementary evaluation package, including the updated simulation and hardware
materials, has also been updated.

%==============================

%==============================
\begin{shaded*}
\noindent \textbf{Q:}
{\itshape
Strengths:
- Coherent end-to-end framework; The integration of WCCG, gap ranking,
pushing-mode generation, and physics validation is well designed. The
method jointly considers which obstacle to move and how to push it,
while validating candidate actions through simulation.

- Recursive pushing; The planner can first clear movable blockers in
the swept region and then return to the original gap-opening task. Good
feature!

- Scalability: The method is evaluated with up to 45 movable obstacles,
which is a useful demonstration of scalability in dense clutter. The
reported success remains at 100\% at 45 obstacles, although the
experiment uses five seeds per density.

- Strong simulation results and real-world validation: The proposed
method substantially outperforms the reported baselines in simulation
and is also demonstrated with two real robots.
}
\end{shaded*}

\noindent \textbf{A:}
We thank the reviewer for these observations. The revision keeps the
end-to-end structure unchanged and strengthens the three aspects that the
revision touches: the recursive-pushing mechanism is now illustrated by its own
figure (Fig.~4, Page~4) and remains a distinguishing feature of the planner; the
dense-clutter evaluation now uses ten independent seed sequences per density
instead of five (Sec.~IV-A6, Page~7), which addresses the caveat attached to the
scalability result; and the two-robot hardware demonstration, including the WCCG
overlays of the executed runs, is retained in Fig.~11 (Page~8) with the execution
monitor now specified explicitly.

%==============================
\begin{shaded*}
\noindent \textbf{Q:}
{\itshape
Physics-model robustness is insufficiently characterized: The method
relies heavily on simulation-based feasibility validation, but
sensitivity to uncertainty in mass, friction, force limits, contact
dynamics etc. is not systematically evaluated.
}
\end{shaded*}

\noindent \textbf{A:}
We agree that this was the most substantive gap, because the feasibility
validation is simulation based. The original hardware experiments demonstrated
closed-loop recovery but did not \emph{isolate} sensitivity to individual model
parameters. The revision therefore adds a dedicated planner--execution
mismatch study designed to separate model mismatch from model re-identification:

\begin{itemize}
\item The planner always uses the nominal
model; \emph{only} the execution simulator is perturbed. The experiment
therefore measures the consequence of an incorrect planning model rather than
the benefit of re-identifying it.
\item Obstacle mass, lateral
friction, spinning friction and the robot force limit are scaled separately by
$\{0.6,0.8,1.2,1.4\}$, i.e.\ $\pm20\%$ and $\pm40\%$, using the same scene
seeds as the nominal condition.
\end{itemize}

\noindent Across 17 conditions ($M{=}30$, $N{=}2$, 10 seeds each, 170 trials),
PushAround succeeds in \textbf{166/170 (97.6\%)}. The perturbed subset contains
\textbf{156/160 successes (97.5\%)}: the aggregate rate is 78/80 (97.5\%) at
$\pm20\%$ and 78/80 (97.5\%) at $\pm40\%$, compared with 10/10 nominal. The
four failures comprise one mid-execution planning failure (lateral friction
$\times0.6$) and three trials that exceeded the per-trial budget (spin friction
$\times1.2$, force limit $\times0.6$ and $\times1.2$). Mean replans increase
from $2.3$ (nominal) to $3.9$ (averaged over the $\pm40\%$ conditions; up to
$8.1$ under force limit $\times0.6$), while mean execution time increases from
$104.7$\,s to $159.6$\,s in the latter condition.

\noindent For context, SL-Push (sim) and DFS-WCCG were also evaluated under
nominal planning and execution-only perturbations, using five seeds per
condition. Under the eight $\pm40\%$ extremes, SL-Push (sim) succeeds in
\textbf{13/40 trials (32.5\%)} and DFS-WCCG in \textbf{23/40 trials (57.5\%)}.
Their mean planning times are $31.5$\,s and $229.1$\,s, respectively, compared
with $18.4$\,s for PushAround. Because the baseline supplement uses fewer seeds
and has lower nominal success (3/5 for each baseline), these results provide
end-to-end context rather than a normalized estimate of each method's
degradation from its own nominal performance.

\begin{shaded*}
\noindent \textbf{Q:}
{\itshape
The experiments primarily use two robots. What happens when the
number of robots increases with the complexity of the task? How does
the search and the contact/force formulation scale to larger robot
teams?
}
\end{shaded*}

\noindent \textbf{A:}
This comment revealed a bottleneck in the shared mode-generation module. We
added two local refinements and re-ran PushAround and both baselines with the
same updated generator; the resulting comparison is reported below.

For complexity, with $|\mathcal A|$ reachable contact candidates per robot, an
exhaustive joint assignment would require $|\mathcal A|^N$ tuples. The greedy
stage instead tests at most $N|\mathcal A|$ single-contact substitutions, each
solving a force LP with a $2N$-dimensional decision vector; the bounded
backtracking stage adds only its specified finite budget. Larger teams therefore
increase both the contact-assignment and LP costs, even though the full joint
assignment space is not enumerated. Sec.~III-E2 states this distinction.

The preliminary $N{=}4$ runs failed in 3 of 10 seeds (2 planning failures and
1 budget timeout). The diagnosis was that, for
some push tasks \emph{no} full-team contact assignment exists --- small
pushable faces, or the pairwise pusher-clearance constraint rejecting every
remaining candidate of one robot --- and the legacy greedy replacement was
additionally sensitive to robot ordering. We added two mode-generation-local
refinements that leave global planning, task sampling and the search tree
unchanged: (i) \emph{bounded backtracking} contact assignment and (ii) a
\emph{temporary per-task robot release} (up to one robot is excluded for that
single push and holds its pose, a penalty is charged during mode ranking so
full-team modes are preferred, and the full team is re-available for every
later task).

Both refinements are in the shared mode generator, so the baseline cells were
re-run with the same code. Across their 40 trial logs, instrumentation records
$20{,}890$ failed greedy replacements, $10{,}904$ bounded-backtracking
assignments (of which $739$ rescue a failed greedy replacement), and $6{,}017$
per-task robot releases. These counts confirm that the added paths are exercised

\begin{shaded*}
\noindent \textbf{Q:}
{\itshape
What guarantees, if any, does the hybrid search provide regarding
solution quality?
}
\end{shaded*}

\noindent \textbf{A:}
We appreciate this question. No global optimality or completeness guarantee is
claimed, and the revision now states this explicitly in the formulation,
node-selection, and termination discussions.

PIHS samples a finite set of pushing directions and contact modes, uses bounded
gap lookahead, ranks nodes with a heuristic best-first priority whose
remaining-cost term is \emph{not} required to be admissible, and terminates at
the first physics-validated $W$-clear solution. The returned schedule is
therefore a feasible schedule under the evaluation model, not a globally
minimal one. Eq.~(5) is consequently presented as the clearance cost that ranks
candidate schedules; it does not turn the implemented search into a solver of
the underlying continuous hybrid optimization problem.

Completeness is also not guaranteed: a feasible scene may require an unsampled
pushing direction, contact assignment, or deeper recursion, or may exceed the
finite computation budget. The node-expansion counts and ablations characterize
empirical search efficiency and solution-finding reliability; they are not
guarantees of solution quality.

\noindent\textbf{Changes in the revised manuscript.} Sec.~II-D (Page~2), the
node-selection discussion (Page~5), and the termination discussion (Page~5)
now describe Eq.~(5) as a ranking cost and explicitly state the limitations
introduced by finite sampling, bounded lookahead, a non-admissible heuristic,
and first-feasible termination:
\begin{itemize}
\item The formulation now describes Eq.~(5) as ranking feasible schedules and
limits the claim to simulator-validated feasibility.
\item The search and termination text state that finite sampling and a finite
computation budget do not guarantee completeness.
\item The node-selection text identifies the remaining-cost term as a
non-admissible heuristic and makes clear that the priority does not provide an
A* optimality guarantee.
\end{itemize}

revision replaces the phrase with an explicit state-based monitor.

In the hardware experiments, motion capture provides robot and object poses. A
push is declared to deviate --- and replanning is triggered from the observed
state --- when the measured object pose departs from the successor state
predicted by $\mathsf{EvalSim}$ by more than \textbf{0.3\,m in position} or

task predicate, invalidated WCCG connectivity for the remaining schedule).
The pose threshold is deliberately loose: a slightly larger or slightly
off-axis push is accepted rather than aborted, because such deviations are
routinely recoverable by the subsequent tasks. No onboard force sensing or
mass/friction estimation is required.

\begin{shaded*}
\noindent \textbf{Q:}
{\itshape
First, the WCCG connectivity condition is important since it is used
throughout the search and also determines termination. The intuition is
reasonably clear, but I think the paper would benefit from a little
more justification here, or perhaps a comparison with a standard

\noindent \textbf{A:}
We agree, and we provide both the requested comparison and an empirical
validation. Standard alternatives first inflate the obstacles by $W/2$ and then
test connectivity on an occupancy grid (BFS/A* or a distance transform) or on a
sampling-based roadmap (PRM/RRT-style). These approaches approximate the answer
at a chosen grid resolution or sample density and generally return a binary
connectivity result; the representation must also be updated as obstacles move.
WCCG instead builds an embedded graph directly from the clearance-relevant
geometry. Its annotated bridges expose the separating bottleneck and frontier
sequence that PIHS uses to choose the next obstacle, in addition to answering
the connectivity query. The same-face test is the graph query used to evaluate
the terminal condition; its validity follows from the $W/2$ inflation
interpretation under the stated decomposition. Non-convex obstacles are handled
by the clearance-relevant convex decomposition described in Sec.~III-A2
(Page~3).

practice, we compare WCCG against two independent references on 300 random
scenes stratified into convex, mixed, and non-convex obstacles: a continuous

continuous reference and produces \textbf{zero false positives and zero false

terminate PIHS prematurely. WCCG answers a query in \textbf{1.07}\,ms on
average, compared with $1.36$\,ms for CC and $243.1$\,ms for FR. The latter
ratio is an implementation-level $\mathbf{227\times}$ speed difference, and

\noindent \textbf{Q:}

Second, I was a little confused about the relationship between Eq.~(5)

\noindent \textbf{Q:}
{\itshape
Third, it is not completely clear how the optimized contact forces are

\noindent \textbf{A:}
Thank you for raising both points. The optimized forces are not commanded on
the hardware; they are used to certify wrench feasibility, while the hardware
tracks a short-horizon object-motion reference generated by the simulator.

For a candidate contact tuple, the LP checks whether the robots can generate a
wrench compatible with the desired object motion within the modeled force,
friction, and unilateral-contact constraints. The optimized force vector is
therefore a feasibility and ranking variable, not a hardware setpoint. Over the
next control interval, $\mathsf{EvalSim}$ predicts a short forward reference
(a few centimetres and a few degrees); the velocity-level controller converts
this reference and the motion-capture pose error into planar velocity commands
for the Mecanum UGVs. Feedback and replanning absorb the difference between the
reference and the realized motion.

Coupled motion is handled by the rollout. Although a high-level strategy names
one directly targeted obstacle, $\mathsf{EvalSim}$ advances all robots and all
movable bodies jointly; object--object contacts are resolved by the physics
engine and the complete successor scene is returned to the search. The adjacent
T-shaped block in hardware Scenario~2 therefore moves as a contact-induced
outcome rather than as a separately scripted task.
