The authors introduce PushAround, a hybrid search framework informed by
physics, aimed at cooperative path clearing among many movable
obstacles. Although the setting is rooted in Navigation Among Movable
Obstacles (NAMO), the focus here is distinctive: several mobile robots
work together to push obstacles aside and carve out a corridor wide
enough for a bigger external vehicle to pass. The pipeline brings
together WCCG-based geometric analysis, gap ranking, generation of
pushing modes, and physics-based validation in simulation. Experiments
span simulated scenes with as many as 45 movable obstacles, plus a
hardware demonstration using two robots.

What I Liked
- A well-integrated pipeline.
The pieces fit together convincingly: WCCG, gap ranking, pushing-mode
generation, and physics checking operate as one system. I particularly
appreciate that the planner decides both which obstacle to move and how
to push it, and that each candidate action is tested in simulation
before it is adopted.

- Recursive pushing.
When a blocker sits in the swept region, the planner can move it first
and then resume the original gap-opening objective. This is a nice
design choice that makes the approach more flexible than single-step
pushing.

- Evidence of scaling to clutter.
Handling up to 45 obstacles is a meaningful stress test. The method
reportedly achieves full success even at that density, though this
result rests on only five seeds per density level, which I address
below.

- Convincing results in simulation and on hardware.
The approach clearly beats the baselines that are reported, and the
two-robot real-world experiment shows the idea can work outside
simulation.

Concerns
- Unclear robustness to model error.
Because the whole approach leans on simulation to judge whether a push
is feasible, its reliability depends on how accurate the physical model
is. Yet there is no systematic study of how errors or uncertainty in
mass, friction, force limits, or contact behavior affect performance.
For a method whose central claim is physics-informed planning, this is
an important omission.

- Thin statistical evidence at high complexity.
The densest scenarios are tested with relatively few independent runs.
A perfect success rate over five seeds is encouraging but offers
limited confidence, and it undercuts the scalability argument somewhat.


This is a solid and well-presented paper. Combining geometric
reasoning, recursive pushing, and physics-informed search is an
interesting direction, and the hardware validation adds credibility. My
main reservations are the missing sensitivity analysis for physical
parameters and the small number of trials in the densest settings.
Resolving these points, and answering the questions above, would
considerably strengthen the submission.
